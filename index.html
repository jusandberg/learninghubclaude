LESSON 1

Why water matters

Water is the environment in which much of the body's chemistry happens. Your body isn't a collection of dry organs — cells exist in fluid environments, and blood, digestive secretions, urine, sweat, lymph and tears all contain water.

Think of your body as a city. Food provides the materials. Oxygen helps provide the energy. Organs are the buildings and factories. But without a transportation system, materials can't get where they need to go, and waste can't leave. Water is that transportation system.

PART B · CARBOHYDRATES

What is a carbohydrate?

Carbohydrates are one of the three macronutrients — carbohydrate, protein, fat. Alive wants you to associate them primarily with energy, but that's not their only role: they also provide fibre, contribute to the functioning of internal organs, and help regulate the metabolism of the other macronutrients.

The test asks which is not a function of carbohydrates, with "encourages ketogenesis" as the contrasting option — ketogenesis happens when carbohydrate intake is low, so it isn't something carbs themselves do.

CARBS = FUEL + FIBRE

TYPES OF CARBOHYDRATES

Sugars, starches & fibre

The book organizes carbohydrates around three principal categories: sugars, starches, and fibre. Within sugars, building blocks matter more than memorizing names.

🧠 The -ose / -ase trick: "-ose" usually marks a sugar, "-ase" usually marks the enzyme that breaks it down. Lactose is the sugar, lactase is the enzyme; sucrose is the sugar, sucrase is the enzyme. Alive spends real time on lactose specifically — it appears in breast milk, and lactase is the enzyme responsible for digesting it.

Polysaccharides ("poly" = many) are long chains — think starch. Picture a pearl necklace: digestion cuts the chain down, piece by piece, until only single, absorbable units remain.

GLYCEMIC INDEX

GI = speed, not sweetness

Alive asks this directly: the glycemic index determines how quickly a food raises blood sugar levels — not how much sugar or how many calories it contains.

Lower GI

🥔 The potato/ice cream surprise: Alive's true/false item — "potatoes are lower on the glycemic index than ice cream" — exists specifically to challenge "sweet taste = higher GI." GI isn't a sweetness scale; food structure, starch type, processing, and mixed nutrients all change the glycemic response.
FIBRE

Soluble vs. insoluble

Fibre is carbohydrate, but unlike digestible carbohydrate, much of it isn't broken down into glucose by human digestive enzymes.

💧

Soluble

Interacts with water, may form viscous gels (oats, legumes, some fruits). Can influence stool characteristics, glucose absorption, and cholesterol metabolism — hemicellulose compounds like glucomannans and galactomannans are specifically tested for cholesterol-lowering activity.
🌾

Insoluble

Adds bulk and supports bowel function (whole grains, many vegetables).
SOLUBLE → SOAKS · INSOLUBLE → BULK

Fibre connects carbs to digestion → bowel function → gut microbiota → metabolic health → cardiovascular health. That's why "carbohydrates are bad" is nutritionally useless — fruit, beans, oats, and table sugar are all carbohydrate, but not remotely interchangeable. Alive itself tests that carbohydrates are not healthy in every form.

GLYCOGEN, LIPOGENESIS & LIPOLYSIS

Storage, building, and breaking down

If glucose is available but not needed immediately, some is stored as glycogen — the body's quick-access carbohydrate storage, mainly in liver and muscle.

🧈

Lipogenesis

LIPO = fat, GENESIS = create. The conversion of excess glucose into fatty acids and triglycerides that can be stored as body fat.
🔥

Lipolysis

LIPO = fat, LYSIS = break apart. Converting stored fat reserves back into fatty acids for fuel.

Case 1 — Meet Sarah, the gym client

▾

Sarah is 44. She exercises five days a week and says: "I drink eight glasses of water every day, so my hydration should be perfect."

Your practitioner brain asks:

  • How active are you?
  • How much do you sweat?
  • What is your environment like?
  • What does your diet look like?
  • What other fluids do you consume?
  • Are there health/medication considerations needing referral?
Activity ↑ → sweat/fluid losses may ↑ → water requirements can change. A universal number doesn't tell the whole story.

Case 2 — "Which filter should I buy?"

▾

Maria says: "I'm worried about chemicals in my water. Should I buy a carbon filter, RO system, or distiller?"

Practitioner thinking: the first question is what are you trying to remove? Carbon ≠ RO ≠ distillation.

A much more responsible answer starts with identifying the concern, not naming a favourite technology.

Case 3 — More water must be better

▾

Jennifer has read online that "detoxing" requires drinking huge quantities of water, and has dramatically increased her intake.

This unit should make you uncomfortable with more water = more health. Individual requirements matter, and water exists in relationship with electrolytes and physiology.

You wouldn't diagnose or treat a potential electrolyte disorder as a nutrition practitioner — an important scope habit to build now.

Carbohydrates come up constantly in real conversations too — clients rarely ask "explain glycolysis," they say "carbs make you fat" or "my blood sugar crashes." You need something more sophisticated than CARBS = GOOD or CARBS = BAD: type + amount + food source + context + person.

Case 4 — "Carbs make me gain weight"

▾

Amanda, 47, says: "I'm going through menopause, so I've completely stopped eating carbohydrates."

Instead of arguing, investigate — what carbs did she remove?

  • Candy? Sugary drinks? Pastries?
  • Or fruit, beans, oats, potatoes, whole grains?
What foods were removed → what nutrients disappeared with them → what happened to fibre → what replaced those foods → is the diet sustainable? Much better than debating "carbs."

Case 5 — The 3 PM crash

▾

Michelle, 43: coffee for breakfast, a refined bagel + sweetened drink for lunch, then at 3pm — "I'm starving and exhausted and I desperately want sugar."

Meal composition → carbohydrate quality → protein? → fibre? → fat? → satiety? → eating pattern? You're not diagnosing hypoglycemia from a symptom — you're investigating the nutrition pattern.

Case 6 — Constipation on low-carb

▾

Lisa, 51: "I'm eating low-carb because it's healthier." Her diet has very little fruit, few legumes, almost no whole grains, limited vegetables — and she reports constipation.

Fibre + fluid + movement + overall diet. Don't jump to a supplement — first understand what the diet is actually providing.

FAT IS ENERGY DENSE

9 kcal/g — spot the exam trap

Carbohydrate and protein each provide about 4 kcal/g. Fat provides about 9 kcal/g — more than twice as much energy per gram.

🍞Carb: 4 kcal/g
🥩Protein: 4 kcal/g
🥑Fat: 9 kcal/g
Spot the trap
A quiz option claims fat "packs more than three times" the calories of protein or carbs. Is that accurate?
True — 9 is more than three times 4
False — 9 ÷ 4 = 2.25, so just over twice as much

3A · WHAT EXACTLY IS A VITAMIN?

Organic micronutrients, not fuel

Vitamins are organic micronutrients needed in small quantities for normal physiological function — essential for growth, vitality, and health, and important as coenzymes. Vitamins do not provide energy. Alive tests this directly: "vitamins are sources of energy" is the false statement.

Carbohydrate ≈ 4 kcal/g, protein ≈ 4 kcal/g, fat ≈ 9 kcal/g. Vitamins: 0 kcal. Many B vitamins become components of coenzymes involved in reactions that let the body metabolize carbs, fats, and proteins — that's why "B vitamins give you energy" is a mix-up between providing energy and participating in energy metabolism.

4A · WHAT ARE MINERALS?

Inorganic, but essential

Minerals do not provide energy — but they help the body produce and use it. No magnesium calories, no calcium calories, no iron calories, but without them metabolism doesn't function normally. Alive tests mineral involvement in energy production, fluid regulation, and acid-base balance.

An essential mineral is one the body needs but cannot make itself — the same idea as essential amino acids or essential fatty acids. Essential ≠ "extra important"; it means the body needs an external (dietary) source.

🍽️ Food first: Alive emphasizes a variety of wholesome foods (with a preference for organic/local produce and grains) as the healthiest approach to optimal mineral levels. Food first, supplements when justified — because minerals interact, and more calcium, zinc, or iron isn't always better.

UNDER THE HOOD · WHY HERBS INTERACT WITH MEDICATIONS

It's not magic — it's shared machinery

"Natural" supplements interact with drugs through the same ordinary biochemical machinery drugs use on each other. Click each mechanism:

⚙️

Liver enzyme competition

Many drugs are broken down by a family of liver enzymes (CYP450). Some herbs (St. John's Wort is a classic example) can speed up or slow down those same enzymes, changing how fast a medication is cleared — making it too weak or too strong.
🩸

Additive effects

Two substances that both thin the blood (say, an anticoagulant medication and high-dose vitamin E or fish oil) can add together, even though neither alone would be a problem at their usual dose.
🧪

Protein-binding competition

Some drugs travel through blood attached to carrier proteins. A compound competing for that same binding site can temporarily raise the amount of "free" active drug in circulation.
🔄

Opposing physiology

A supplement that pushes a system one direction (e.g., licorice's effect on blood pressure/potassium) can work against a medication designed to push it the other way.
🩺 This is why "it's just an herb" isn't a safety argument — the question is always which mechanism, which medication, and what dose. This is also why the supplement-safety checklist in 5J always asks about medications specifically, not just "any side effects?"

UNDER THE HOOD · HOW FIBRE FERMENTATION ACTUALLY WORKS

Fibre → microbes → butyrate → colonocyte fuel

Section 6B mentioned short-chain fatty acids — here's the mechanism in more depth. Click each step:

1
🌾 Fibre that resists human digestive enzymes reaches the colon largely intact.
2
🦠 Colon bacteria ferment it, extracting energy for themselves in the process.
3
⚗️ Fermentation byproducts include short-chain fatty acids — acetate, propionate, and butyrate.
4
🔋 Butyrate is preferentially used as fuel by the cells lining the colon itself (colonocytes) — different from small-intestine cells, which rely more on glutamine.
5
🩺 Adequate colonocyte fuel supports the gut lining's structure and function over time.
🔗 This is the mechanism behind why fibre variety (not just fibre quantity) matters — different fibre types feed different bacterial populations, which produce different short-chain fatty acid profiles.
UNDER THE HOOD · WHAT "COMPLETE PROTEIN" ACTUALLY MEASURES

Beyond the grain + legume rule of thumb

Section 6J covered quinoa as a complete protein and the grain + legume complementary pairing. The modern practitioner concept underneath "complete protein":

🧬

Amino acid profile

A protein is "complete" if it supplies all nine essential amino acids in reasonable proportions relative to human needs — not just "contains all of them in trace amounts."
📊

Digestibility matters too

Modern protein-quality scoring (concepts like PDCAAS/DIAAS) accounts for how well a protein is actually digested and absorbed, not just its amino acid content on paper — which is why food form and preparation can matter.
🩺 Practitioner takeaway: don't reduce plant protein adequacy to a single "complete vs. incomplete" label for one food. Look at the whole day's protein variety and total intake — the same conclusion as the grain + legume rule, arrived at with more nuance.

M2–M3 · FOOD → NUTRIENTS → ENERGY

Follow the fuel

After a carbohydrate-containing meal, blood glucose rises, pancreatic beta cells respond, and insulin rises to help cells store/use that glucose. Between meals, glucagon helps the liver make glucose available.

INSULIN = STORE/USE THE FUEL · GLUCAGON = MAKE FUEL AVAILABLE

Protein undergoes continuous turnover — synthesis and breakdown happening at once — which is why dietary protein matters throughout life, not just for muscle-building. Fat takes a different absorption route: most carbs and amino acids travel via the portal circulation to the liver, but long-chain dietary fat goes intestine → lymph → blood as chylomicrons.

⚡ Ultimately, cells capture usable energy largely as ATP — food doesn't contain little packets of electricity. Many vitamins and minerals participate as coenzymes/cofactors that let those metabolic reactions proceed. B vitamins don't provide calories — they help the body use energy-yielding nutrients.

7I · EMOTIONS + OUR RELATIONSHIP WITH FOOD

Hunger isn't the only reason humans eat

We eat because of pleasure, comfort, celebration, stress, fatigue, social situations, habit, environment, culture, sensory cues, availability — and yes, physiological hunger.

🚨 Don't learn "emotional eating is bad." Eating for pleasure or comfort is sometimes normal. The real question: is this pattern causing distress or working against the client's goals? 7J · FOOD COMBINING

A major Alive vs. evidence section

Alive teaches: fruit should be eaten alone; proteins shouldn't be mixed with starches; meat needs an acidic medium; starch needs an alkaline environment; fruit with other foods may "ferment" in the body.

🔴 Know this is in the course. Do not build your practice around this theory.
